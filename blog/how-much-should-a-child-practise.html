How much should a child practise chess?

Less than most parents imagine, and more regularly. Here is a realistic weekly load by age and level.

For parentsImprovement

The honest starting point: for most children, the right amount of chess practice is considerably less than an ambitious parent would like, and considerably more consistent.

A realistic weekly load

Ages 5 to 7, learning: one coached session a week, plus perhaps ten minutes of puzzles on two or three other days. That is enough. Attention at this age is the limiting factor, not time.

Ages 8 to 11, playing club chess: one or two coached sessions, fifteen minutes of puzzles most days, and one slow game a week. Around two to three hours total.

Competing juniors: four to six hours a week, and the composition matters more than the number. Roughly half should be studying, a third playing slow games, and the rest reviewing.

Why little and often wins

Pattern recognition is the core skill in chess, and patterns are built by repeated exposure over time, not by cramming. Fifteen minutes of puzzles on six days does more than ninety minutes on one.

It also protects the thing that matters most, which is that the child still wants to do it next month.

What the time should be spent on

Ranked by value per minute for an improving junior:

  1. Tactics puzzles. Highest return, by a distance.
  2. Reviewing their own losses. Uncomfortable, and the fastest way to stop repeating a mistake.
  3. Slow games. Where the skills actually get used.
  4. Endgame study. Undervalued. Endgames are finite and learnable, unlike openings.
  5. Opening study. Useful eventually, wildly over-prioritised by beginners.

Notice that memorising openings, which is what most self-directed children do, sits at the bottom.

Signs of too much

  • Chess is being negotiated rather than chosen.
  • They play fast to get it over with.
  • They stop wanting to look at losses.
  • Results go sideways while hours go up.

If you see these, cut the volume. A child who takes a month off and returns keen will out-improve one who is grinding resentfully.

Signs of too little

  • They cannot remember what they worked on last week.
  • The same mistake recurs across months.
  • Puzzles feel hard in a way they did not three months ago, because nothing is being consolidated.

The number that actually predicts progress

Not hours. Days per week on which they touch chess at all. A child doing twenty minutes on five days will pass a child doing two hours on one, almost every time.

Consistency is the whole game, and it is also the easiest thing for a parent to help with.
