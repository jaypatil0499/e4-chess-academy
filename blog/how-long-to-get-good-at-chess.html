How long does it take to get good at chess?

A realistic timeline for a child starting from zero, and why the honest answer depends on what you mean by good.

For parentsImprovement

Parents ask this in the first conversation, and the honest answer starts with a question back: good compared to whom? Beating a parent, beating a school club, and getting a national rating are three different projects with three different timelines.

Beating the family: three to six months

For a child playing once a week with a coach and a little practice between, beating most adult family members takes a few months. Casual adult players usually stopped learning after they picked up the rules, so a child who knows basic tactics overtakes them quickly.

This is the milestone that convinces a child chess is worth doing, which is why we do not delay tactics.

Holding their own at a school club: six to twelve months

School clubs are a real jump. There will be one or two children who play seriously. Reaching the point where your child wins some and loses some usually takes a year of consistent work.

A first rating: one to two years

Playing rated tournaments and getting a published FIDE rating is realistic inside two years for a child who enjoys competing. The rating itself will be modest and that is normal.

Genuinely strong: years, and it stops being about time

Beyond that, timelines break down, because progress stops depending on hours and starts depending on quality of work. A child studying endgames for two hours a week will pass a child playing blitz for ten.

What actually moves the needle

Consistency beats intensity. Two sessions a week for a year does more than a summer of daily chess followed by nothing.

Reviewing losses. The strongest predictor we see. Children who look at why they lost improve roughly twice as fast as children who play the next game immediately.

Playing stronger opponents. Comfortable wins teach almost nothing.

Slow games. Blitz is fun and it is where most children spend their time, but rapid improvement comes from games long enough to actually think.

The honest caveat

None of this is a schedule, and children do not progress in straight lines. A child can plateau for four months and then jump two hundred rating points in a term because something clicked. Plateaus are not evidence of a ceiling.

The one thing that reliably stops progress is a child who has stopped enjoying it. If practice has become a chore being enforced by a parent, the timeline stretches out regardless of how good the coaching is.
