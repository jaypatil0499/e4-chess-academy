The honest answer is that age is the wrong thing to measure. We have taught five-year-olds who sat through a full lesson and eight-year-olds who could not. What matters is whether a child is ready, and readiness shows up in ways that have nothing to do with chess.

## Three signs a child is ready

**They can lose a game without it ruining the afternoon.** This is the big one. Chess involves losing, constantly, including to people they like. A child who can shake that off will enjoy chess. A child who cannot will need a gentler introduction, and that is fine.

**They can sit with one thing for fifteen minutes.** Not an hour. Fifteen minutes of genuine attention on a puzzle, a construction toy, or a story is enough to begin with.

**They ask why.** Chess rewards curiosity more than memory. A child who wants to know why a move was bad will improve faster than one who memorises openings.

## What five-year-olds can actually learn

Quite a lot, as long as it arrives in the right order. In the first month a five-year-old can reliably learn how each piece moves, what check means, and how to get out of it. What they usually cannot do yet is plan two moves ahead, and pushing that too early is how children decide chess is not for them.

The mistake we see most often is starting with openings. An opening is a memorised sequence, and memorising is the least interesting thing chess has to offer. Start with how the pieces move and the small tactics that make a child feel clever.

## Starting later is not a disadvantage

Parents often worry that beginning at nine or ten means their child has missed the window. They have not. Older beginners learn the rules in days rather than weeks, hold longer plans in their head, and read notation almost immediately. A ten-year-old starting now will pass a six-year-old who started last year, more often than not.

What changes with age is motivation. Young children play because it is fun. Older children want to see themselves getting better, which means ratings, tournaments and a visible track record matter more.

## What a first lesson should look like

A good first session should involve playing, not lecturing. If your child spends thirty minutes listening to someone talk about chess, something has gone wrong. They should touch the pieces in the first five minutes.

They should also finish the session able to do something they could not do before, even if it is very small. Getting a king out of check is an achievement for a beginner. Naming all the pieces is an achievement.

## The short version

If your child is five or older, can handle losing, and asks questions, they are ready. If they are three and pushing the pieces around a board, let them. If they are eleven and just got interested, they have not missed anything.

The one thing we would say to any parent: try a lesson before buying a course. Watching how a coach talks to your child for half an hour tells you more than any curriculum document.
