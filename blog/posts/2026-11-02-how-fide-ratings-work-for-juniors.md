A FIDE rating is a number that estimates playing strength, maintained by the International Chess Federation. Parents usually meet it for the first time when their child enters a rated tournament, and it raises more questions than it answers.

## What the number means

A rating is comparative, not absolute. It predicts results between two players: the larger the gap, the more likely the higher-rated player wins. A 200-point gap means the stronger player is expected to win roughly three games in four.

That is all it does. It is not a measure of intelligence, potential, or how much a child has learned. A child can improve substantially and see their rating stay flat, because their opponents improved too.

## How a child gets one

Ratings come only from **FIDE-rated tournaments**, which are events registered with the federation and played under official time controls with an arbiter present. Games at home, online play, and club evenings do not count.

To get a first published rating, a player needs a minimum number of games against already-rated opponents, achieved across one or more rated events. Until then they are unrated, which simply means there is not yet enough evidence.

This is why the first tournament matters more administratively than competitively. The result barely matters. Getting into the system does.

## Why junior ratings move quickly

After each rated game, a player's rating adjusts based on the result and on how surprising it was. Beating a much stronger opponent moves the number a lot; beating a much weaker one barely moves it at all.

How fast it moves also depends on a multiplier, which is set higher for new and for young players. The reasoning is straightforward: a child's strength is genuinely changing month to month, so the system is allowed to update its estimate faster. An adult who has been stable at the same level for a decade has their rating move slowly, because it is probably already correct.

The practical consequence is that a junior who is improving can gain a lot of rating in a single good tournament, and lose a chunk in a bad one. Both are normal.

## What parents should watch instead

Rating is a lagging indicator, and it is noisy over short periods. Over a single tournament it tells you almost nothing.

More useful signals:

- **Quality of losses.** Losing narrowly to stronger players is progress. Losing to blunders is not, whatever the rating says.
- **Time management.** Children who improve stop losing on the clock.
- **Whether they review their own games.** The single strongest predictor we see of a child who will keep improving.

A rating that goes sideways for six months while a child stops hanging pieces is a child getting better.

## The one thing not to do

Do not set a rating target as the goal. It is outside a child's direct control, it depends on who they happened to be paired against, and treating it as the scoreboard turns a game into an exam.

Aim at the things they can control: preparation, clock discipline, reviewing losses. The rating follows.
