A beginner with a queen and king against a lone king will win that position perhaps half the time. The other half ends in stalemate, which is a draw, which after twenty moves of chasing feels considerably worse than losing.

## The definitions

**Checkmate:** the king is in check, and there is no legal move to stop it. The game is won.

**Stalemate:** the king is **not** in check, but the player to move has no legal move at all. The game is drawn.

That is the entire difference. In check plus no moves is a win. Not in check plus no moves is a draw.

## Why it happens

Stalemate almost always arrives in winning positions, which is what makes it so painful. The stronger side has a queen, chases the enemy king to the edge, takes away every square, and forgets to leave it a move.

The classic version: black king on a8, white queen on b6. The king is not in check. It cannot go to a7, b7 or b8, all covered by the queen. Black has no other pieces. Draw.

Move the queen to c7 instead and it is not stalemate; it is mate next move.

## How to avoid it

**Count their legal moves before you move.** If the answer is zero and they are not in check, pick a different move.

**Use the king.** Beginners try to checkmate with the queen alone. The correct method is to push the enemy king to the edge with the queen, then bring your own king up to support the final blow. The queen alone cannot mate.

**Avoid getting too close too fast.** A queen placed a knight's move away from the enemy king is a common stalemate pattern.

**When you are overwhelmingly ahead, trade into a simple win.** With an extra queen, giving one back to reach an obvious king-and-pawn ending is often the practical choice.

## Using it deliberately

Stalemate is also a defensive resource. If you are losing badly, look for a position where you have no legal moves. Sacrificing your last pieces to reach stalemate is a legitimate and satisfying way to rescue half a point, and strong juniors learn to hunt for it.

Which is the other reason to learn the pattern: if you do not know what stalemate looks like, you will neither avoid it nor find it.

## The drill worth doing

Set up a king and queen against a lone king and mate it, ten times, from different starting squares. Most children go from unreliable to certain in one session. It is the single highest-value drill in beginner chess, because it converts every winning endgame from a coin flip into a win.
