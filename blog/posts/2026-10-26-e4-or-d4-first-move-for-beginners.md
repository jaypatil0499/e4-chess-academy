There are twenty legal first moves in chess and two good ones for a beginner. Pushing the king's pawn two squares, **e4**, or the queen's pawn two squares, **d4**. Everything else is either worse or too subtle to be worth a child's time.

Both moves do the two things an opening move should: they take central space, and they open a line for a piece that was stuck behind a pawn.

## What e4 gives you

e4 opens a diagonal for the bishop on f1 *and* a diagonal for the queen. Two pieces are freed at once, which is why e4 positions develop quickly and tend to become sharp.

The games are open. Pieces come into contact early, tactics appear by move ten, and somebody usually gets checkmated rather than slowly ground down. For a learning player that is ideal, because the feedback is fast and unmistakable. Play a careless move, lose a knight, understand why within the same game.

The cost is that Black gets counterplay just as quickly. Open positions cut both ways.

## What d4 gives you

d4 opens only the dark-squared bishop, but the pawn on d4 is defended by the queen from the moment it lands. That small difference changes everything: d4 positions tend to stay closed longer, with pawns locked against each other and play built slowly around structure.

These games are strategically richer and tactically quieter. They reward planning over calculation.

## Which one for a child

**Start with e4.** Not because it is objectively better, but because it teaches faster.

A child playing open positions gets punished immediately for leaving pieces undefended, for moving the same piece twice, for bringing the queen out early. They learn the rules of opening play by breaking them and losing quickly. In a closed d4 position, the same mistakes take twenty moves to matter, by which point the lesson is lost.

e4 also produces the tactics a beginner needs to see: forks, pins, attacks on f7. Those patterns are the foundation of everything else.

## When to try d4

Once a player has a rating and has stopped hanging pieces, d4 is worth exploring, particularly for children who find sharp positions stressful. Some players are simply more comfortable with a slow build than a knife fight, and forcing a naturally positional child to play wild attacking chess is a good way to make them dislike the game.

## The thing that actually matters

Far more important than which pawn you push is what you do next. The opening principles are the same either way:

- Control the centre.
- Develop knights and bishops before the queen and rooks.
- Castle early.
- Do not move the same piece twice without a reason.
- Do not bring the queen out early, however tempting the trap.

A player who follows those five rules after d4 will beat a player who ignores them after e4, every time.
