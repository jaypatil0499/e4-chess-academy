Nearly every child now learns chess partly online. That is a genuine improvement over a generation ago, when getting a game meant finding someone willing to play. But online and over-the-board chess build different skills, and children who only do one end up lopsided.

## What online chess is good at

**Volume.** A child can play more games in a month online than most players managed in a year before the internet.

**Instant opponents at the right level.** Rating systems pair a child against someone of similar strength within seconds. That is hard to arrange in person.

**Puzzles.** Tactics trainers are genuinely excellent. Pattern recognition is mostly a function of how many patterns you have seen, and online puzzles deliver them efficiently.

**Analysis.** An engine will show a child exactly where a game turned, immediately.

## What it is bad at

**Speed.** Almost all online play is fast. Children build the habit of moving in three seconds, which is precisely the habit that loses tournament games.

**Consequence.** A lost online game costs nothing; there is always another in ten seconds. That produces careless play, and careless play becomes a reflex.

**Touching the pieces.** Children who only play online are noticeably worse at visualising a physical board, and some find their first tournament disorienting.

**Nerves.** Sitting across from an opponent, with a clock and an arbiter, is a different experience. It has to be practised.

## What over-the-board play gives

Slow games, real consequences, and the discipline of writing moves down, which alone slows a child's thinking to a useful pace. It also builds the social half of chess: shaking hands, losing gracefully, sitting still for two hours.

Every strong junior we have coached plays over the board regularly. It is not nostalgia; it is where the skill gets tested.

## A reasonable balance

For a child learning seriously:

- **Puzzles online, most days.** Fifteen minutes is plenty.
- **Some fast online play, for fun.** It keeps them interested.
- **At least one slow game a week**, online with a long time control or in person.
- **A physical board at home**, used during lessons. Setting up a real position while a coach explains it is worth more than watching a screen.
- **A tournament every few months**, once they are ready.

## The one rule worth enforcing

If a child plays only three-minute games, no amount of coaching will fix the resulting habits. The single most useful change most parents can make is to insist on some slow chess.
